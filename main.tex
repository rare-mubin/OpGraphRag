\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a highly successful paradigm to alleviate factual hallucinations and ground Large Language Models (LLMs) in external document collections. However, traditional vector RAG typically operates on chunk-based linear document slices that lack structural semantic connections and struggle with complex multi-hop queries. To address this, GraphRAG structures unstructured text as entity-relation graphs and hierarchical community summaries to support comprehensive global reasoning \cite{edge2024local}. Concurrently, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has shown great potential in training language models to make sequential decisions and navigate complex retrieval spaces . By formulating retrieval and reasoning as Markov Decision Processes and optimizing policies using environmental task-level rewards, RL provides a dynamic optimization mechanism to adapt static graph structures for downstream utility \cite{tsang2026autographr1}.

A rich line of research has explored learning-based graph reduction, sparsification, and context compression to handle the scale of complex graphs . In graph reduction, frameworks such as SparRL and Cutter \cite{chai2025cutter} leverage RL to prune non-essential edges and nodes to preserve graph robustness and topological properties \cite{hashemi2024comprehensive} . For context compression, methods like CORE \cite{cui2025core} and DynaRAG \cite{zhang2026dynarag} utilize RL to adaptively compress textual contexts based on downstream task performance . More recently, RL has been integrated directly into knowledge graph construction (AutoGraph-R1 \cite{tsang2026autographr1}) and agentic multi-turn GraphRAG reasoning (Graph-R1 \cite{luo2026graphr1} and GraphRAG-R1 \cite{yu2026graphragr1}) to optimize generation quality, retrieval relevance, and reasoning path correctness.

Despite these substantial advances, existing frameworks suffer from critical bottlenecks. First, traditional graph sparsification and reduction methods remain decoupled from downstream generator requirements, optimizing instead for task-agnostic, intrinsic topological metrics like degree or density. Second, current GraphRAG pipelines incur significant computational and token overhead from encoding entire retrieved subgraphs or dense community summaries at inference time. Third, existing schemas rely on rigid, predefined heuristics or hop counts, failing to adaptively compress graph contexts based on the specific semantic complexity of each query.

To resolve these limitations, we introduce an end-to-end framework for Adaptive Graph Compression using Reinforcement Learning for Efficient GraphRAG. Rather than task-agnostic preprocessing, our framework directly aligns graph reduction with downstream question-answering utility using direct generator feedback. Specifically, we address the core challenges of structural redundancy, massive token overhead, and rigid retrieval schemes. Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Query-Adaptive Graph Compression Policy:} We introduce a reinforcement learning-based graph compression policy that dynamically prunes retrieved subgraphs on the fly, tailoring the residual graph structure to meet the specific semantic demands of each user query.
    \item \textbf{Process-Constrained Joint Reward Mechanism:} We design a joint reward function that integrates downstream question-answering performance with token-level efficiency constraints. This process-constrained mechanism dynamically balances reasoning depth and computational overhead, preventing both shallow retrieval and over-thinking.
    \item \textbf{High-Fidelity Context Compression:} Through extensive experiments on multi-hop benchmarks, we demonstrate that our framework achieves substantial context compression ratios (significantly reducing context lengths at inference time) while maintaining or improving downstream reasoning accuracy over state-of-the-art baselines.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Previous work on Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG), graph reduction and sparsification, and reinforcement learning for context optimization is reviewed in Section \ref{sec:RW}. Section \ref{sec:PS} details the proposed system model, including the Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation for query-adaptive graph compression, the formal definitions of the state and action spaces, and the design of the process-constrained reinforcement learning agent. Section \ref{sec:ERD} presents the experimental setup and analyzes the system's performance specifically focusing on context compression ratios and downstream question-answering accuracy against state-of-the-art baselines on complex multi-hop benchmarks. The limitations of the proposed framework and future research directions are discussed in Section \ref{SEC: limF}, leading to Section \ref{sec:con} which concludes the paper.



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:RW}

The paradigm of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has substantially advanced the applicability of Large Language Models (LLMs) by prepending relevant external documents to the user query, successfully reducing factual hallucinations and grounding model outputs \cite{edge2024local, cui2025core}. However, traditional vector-based RAG approaches are typically restricted to flat, chunk-based document segments that fail to capture high-order semantic relationships among entities, making them ill-suited for complex multi-hop queries or global corpus summarization \cite{luo2026graphr1, edge2024local}. To address these limitations, \citet{edge2024local} pioneered the GraphRAG framework, which constructs structured entity-relationship graphs from raw text and partitions them hierarchically using Leiden community detection. Nonetheless, a major limitation of the approach by \citet{edge2024local} is that the graph construction and community-summarization processes are entirely heuristic and lack a learned compression policy, frequently resulting in severe token bloat and massive computational overhead at inference time. To balance hierarchical retrieval with adaptive semantic integration, \citet{li2026deepgraphrag} proposed Deep GraphRAG to maintain local precision and global context. However, the model designed by \citet{li2026deepgraphrag} applies conciseness or semantic weight rewards primarily to textual summaries rather than performing any adaptive structural compression on the underlying graph topology. Towards dynamic and agentic retrieval pipelines, \citet{luo2026graphr1} proposed Graph-R1, which models the retrieval process as an agentic, multi-turn loop over a knowledge hypergraph. Concurrently, \citet{yu2026graphragr1} developed GraphRAG-R1, which leverages process-constrained reinforcement learning (RL) with progressive retrieval attenuation to guide tool invocation. Despite their reasoning gains, both \citet{luo2026graphr1} and \citet{yu2026graphragr1} focus exclusively on optimizing the step-by-step retrieval navigation policy, leaving the retrieved graph or hypergraph size fixed and uncompressed, which still subjects the downstream generator to significant token overhead.

To handle the scaling bottlenecks associated with large-scale graph databases, graph reduction has emerged as a key preprocessing step to compress networks while preserving vital properties, as detailed in the comprehensive survey by \citet{hashemi2024comprehensive}. However, as a literature survey, the work of \citet{hashemi2024comprehensive} does not propose an empirical methodology of its own nor does it address task-specific downstream generation requirements. In the domain of learning-based sparsification, \citet{wickman2022sparrl} proposed SparRL, which trains deep reinforcement learning agents to prune non-essential edges. Nevertheless, the framework by \citet{wickman2022sparrl} relies on a purely structural reward (e.g., preserving PageRank or community structures) and is not evaluated on any downstream semantic question-answering task, which makes it task-agnostic. Expanding on structural robustness under targeted node-deletion attacks, \citet{chai2025cutter} developed Cutter, which utilizes a dual-agent RL architecture consisting of a node detector and an edge compressor. However, similar to other sparsification baselines, \citet{chai2025cutter} optimize their agents solely for topological connectivity metrics in a structure-only setting, ignoring the semantic and task-specific demands of downstream LLM generators.

Parallel to structural reduction, several works have focused on task-aligned context compression and index optimization. In document context compression, \citet{cui2025core} proposed CORE, an end-to-end framework that leverages Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to condense retrieved text down to 3\% of its original length based on downstream task-level feedback. Similarly, \citet{zhang2026dynarag} developed DynaRAG, employing reinforcement learning to adaptively select context compression ratios according to dynamic query demands. Although highly effective at reducing inference cost, both \citet{cui2025core} and \citet{zhang2026dynarag} operate strictly on flat, unstructured text chunks and are incapable of exploiting or preserving graph-structured topologies. To bridge the gap between graph index building and downstream performance, \citet{tsang2026autographr1} presented AutoGraph-R1, which formulates knowledge graph construction as a policy learning problem optimized via GRPO. However, \citet{tsang2026autographr1} optimize the triple generation process exclusively at construction time; their method lacks a post hoc mechanism to compress the retrieved subgraph size dynamically during query execution. Our work bridges these disparate lines of research by learning a query-adaptive graph compression policy that dynamically prunes retrieved subgraphs on the fly, optimizing downstream generation accuracy and inference token efficiency in unison.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:PS}

The workflow of the proposed adaptive graph compression framework is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}. The proposed system included five main phases: i) dataset, ii) data preprocessing and knowledge graph construction, iii) development of the compression policy, iv) compressed answer generation and reward, and v) evaluation of performance. These phases were incorporated to guarantee reproducibility and the prevention of label leakage in the proposed system.

\subsection{Dataset}
The proposed framework was evaluated using HotpotQA \cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, a multi-hop question-answering benchmark in which each answer requires evidence from more than one document. The distractor development set contains 7,405 questions, each paired with ten context passages, a gold answer, and supporting-fact labels. A subset of 200 hard questions was sampled with a fixed random seed of 42, comprising 170 bridge and 30 comparison questions. The supporting-fact labels identify which retrieved content is required, which makes the dataset suitable for studying compression.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
A preprocessing pipeline was used. First, duplicate passages were removed by title, which produced 1,990 unique passages. Second, the gold answers and supporting-fact labels were kept apart from the passage pool so that no label information entered graph construction. The questions were then allocated to training and validation sets in proportions of 80\% and 20\%, respectively. Since the retrieved subgraph sizes were highly skewed, with a median of 48 nodes and a maximum of 845, the split was stratified by subgraph size. The validation set was used only for checkpoint selection and evaluation.

Entities and relations were extracted from each passage with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \cite{qwen2024technical}, and coreferent mentions were merged through name normalization and model-verified entity resolution, producing a knowledge graph $G=(V,E)$ with 14,797 nodes and 15,391 edges.

\subsection{Proposed Framework}
In the proposed framework, a learned compression stage is placed between retrieval and generation. The query and each node are embedded with BGE-M3 \cite{chen2024bgem3}, and their relevance is measured by cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{sim}(q, v_i) = \frac{E_q \cdot E_i}{\lVert E_q \rVert \, \lVert E_i \rVert},
    \label{eq:sim}
\end{equation}
where $E_q$ and $E_i$ denote the embeddings of the query $q$ and node $v_i$. The five most similar nodes are expanded by two hops to form the retrieved subgraph $G_q$, which covered all supporting passages for 171 of the 200 questions.

Each node in $G_q$ is described by four features: query similarity, normalized degree, normalized hop distance from the nearest seed, and a seed indicator. A multilayer perceptron maps these features to the probability of KEEP, with REMOVE as the alternative action. The policy is trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) \cite{shao2024deepseekmath}, where four trajectories are sampled for each question and compared within the group:
\begin{equation}
    A_i = \frac{R_i - \mu_R}{\sigma_R + \epsilon},
    \label{eq:adv}
\end{equation}
where $R_i$ is the reward of trajectory $i$, $\mu_R$ and $\sigma_R$ are the mean and standard deviation of the group rewards, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant. Because a group reward cannot assign credit to individual nodes, a supervised node-level term is added:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} A_i\,\bar{\ell}_i \;-\; \beta\,\bar{H} \;+\; \eta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}},
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $G=4$, $\bar{\ell}_i$ is the log-probability of trajectory $i$ averaged over nodes so that the gradient scale does not depend on subgraph size, $\bar{H}$ is the policy entropy, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}$ is the class-weighted binary cross-entropy against the supporting-fact labels, with $\beta=0.02$ and $\eta=1.0$.

\subsection{Compressed Answer Generation and Reward}
Compression retains every node whose KEEP probability is at least 0.5, yielding the compressed subgraph $G_c \subseteq G_q$. The retained entities, relations, and source passages are passed to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with a 32,768-token context window to generate the answer $A_c$, while the same procedure applied to $G_q$ produces the uncompressed answer $A_o$. Each training trajectory is rewarded as:
\begin{equation}
    R = Q_c + \lambda\, CR \cdot \mathbb{1}\!\left[Q_c \ge Q_o \wedge Q_c > Q_{\varnothing}\right] - \gamma \max(0,\, Q_o - Q_c),
    \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $Q_c$ and $Q_o$ denote the F1-scores of $A_c$ and $A_o$, $Q_{\varnothing}$ is the F1-score with an empty context, $\lambda=0.5$, and $\gamma=2.0$. The compression bonus is granted only when the compressed answer is at least as good as $A_o$ and better than the answer without context, which prevents rewarding the removal of the entire subgraph on questions the model can answer from parametric knowledge.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
Evaluation of the framework was based on answer quality and compression efficiency. Answer quality was measured by Exact Match (EM) and token-level F1-score, whereas the compression ratio Eq.~(\ref{eq:cr}) reflects the fraction of context tokens removed:
\begin{equation}
    CR = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_o},
    \label{eq:cr}
\end{equation}
where $T_o$ and $T_c$ denote the token counts of the uncompressed and compressed contexts. Each KEEP/REMOVE decision was also evaluated as a binary classification against the supporting-fact labels using precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC. The policy was compared with the uncompressed pipeline and two non-adaptive baselines: similarity pruning, which keeps the 50\% of nodes most similar to the query, and heuristic pruning, which keeps the nodes within one hop of a seed.

\section{Results and Discussions}\label{sec:ERD}

This section focuses on evaluating the learning behavior, the node-level decisions, and the contribution of the state features, as well as a quantitative comparison of the proposed policy with the baselines. All the results were evaluated using the 40 validation questions. As presented in Table~\ref{tab:results}, the proposed policy achieved the highest F1-score of 68.8\%, compared with 63.8\% for the uncompressed pipeline, while reducing the average context from 26,134 to 5,942 tokens. Although heuristic pruning used a similar context of 5,908 tokens, it reached an F1-score of only 63.1\%. This means the policy compressed the context by 58.6\% on average without losing answer quality. This is significant because neither non-adaptive baseline exceeded the uncompressed F1-score.

\subsection{Training and Validation Analysis}
Fig.~\ref{fig:training} summarizes the training behavior of the policy. In Fig.~\ref{fig:training}(a), the compression ratio rises at the start of every epoch because the questions were visited in order of increasing subgraph size, and in the first epoch the smallest quarter of subgraphs was compressed less (0.42) than the rest (0.58 to 0.62). Across epochs, the average compression ratio remained between 0.55 and 0.59 without collapsing toward removing the entire subgraph, and the mean entropy decreased only from 0.600 to 0.529. Fig.~\ref{fig:training}(b) shows that the validation F1-score was highest after the first epoch (0.652) and decreased to 0.610, as recall rose at the expense of precision; therefore, the first-epoch checkpoint was retained.

\subsection{Classification Performance and Confusion Matrix}
Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the confusion matrices of heuristic pruning and the proposed policy on the 4,154 validation nodes, 460 of which are relevant. From the figure, heuristic pruning correctly kept 341 relevant nodes while removing 119 of them. In contrast, the policy correctly kept 382 relevant nodes and removed only 78, at the cost of keeping 330 irrelevant nodes instead of 241. The policy therefore reached a higher recall (0.830 against 0.741) and a lower precision (0.537 against 0.586), with nearly equal F1-scores (0.652 and 0.655) and an AUC of 0.931. This trade-off is consistent with the reward, since removing a supporting node costs more than keeping an irrelevant one.

\subsection{Contribution of the State Features}
The contribution of the state features was examined by training the node classifier with the supervised objective alone and evaluating each representation at the KEEP-rate of heuristic pruning. From Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}, the four structural features alone reached a node-level F1-score of 0.662, whereas adding the 2,048-dimensional node and query embeddings reduced it to 0.518, and projecting them to 64 or 16 dimensions reduced it further to 0.470 and 0.430. Therefore, the raw embeddings add noise at this data scale, and the proposed policy uses only the four structural features.

\subsection{Discussion}
The findings confirm that the policy learned a query-adaptive rule rather than a fixed cut-off. Similar to heuristic pruning, it kept every seed and 95.0\% of the one-hop nodes, but it also selected 4.2\% of the two-hop nodes, recovering 54 of the 119 relevant nodes that any one-hop rule discards. The benefit was concentrated on the 9 questions whose uncompressed context exceeded the 32,768-token window, where the F1-score rose from 0.489 to 0.710, while on the other 31 questions it was unchanged at 0.681. In contrast, heuristic pruning lowered the F1-score by 5.5 points on the 149 of all 200 questions whose context fitted. However, 33 of the 40 validation questions received the same F1-score with and without compression, so the improvement over the uncompressed pipeline was not statistically significant.

\section{Limitations}\label{SEC: limF}

Although the current framework has a strong performance, it bears several limitations that are evident. The policy was developed on 200 HotpotQA questions, and the 40 validation questions were used for both checkpoint selection and evaluation, so no separate test set was available and the answer-level gains were not statistically significant. One quarter of the uncompressed contexts exceeded the 32,768-token window and were truncated. The framework was also evaluated with a single language model and benchmark, and its supervised objective requires supporting-fact labels. Lastly, future research should be done on larger question sets and multiple benchmarks.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:con}

This work elaborates on an adaptive graph compression framework for GraphRAG based on a policy trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization and a supervised node-level objective. Experimental results on 200 questions from the HotpotQA dataset are presented first. The uncompressed GraphRAG pipeline, similarity pruning, and heuristic pruning were employed as baselines and examined within the same experimental framework. After evaluating each of the methods, the proposed policy proved to be the most effective at preserving answer quality under compression, achieving an F1-score of 68.8\% while reducing the context by 58.6\% on average. The analysis also showed that four structural features are sufficient for the policy and that compression is most beneficial when the retrieved context exceeds the window of the language model.

Despite the promising results, there are several directions for future work. Further studies will extend this framework to larger question sets and additional multi-hop benchmarks, evaluate it with other language models, and replace the supporting-fact labels with signals that do not require manual annotation.
