\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation grounded in knowledge graphs offers a promising path toward multi-hop reasoning over large document collections, but encoding entire retrieved subgraphs at inference time imposes substantial token and computational overhead on downstream generators. Existing graph reduction and context compression techniques remain either task-agnostic, relying on fixed topological heuristics, or decoupled from the specific semantic demands of each query, leaving the trade-off between context size and answer quality poorly optimized. We propose a reinforcement learning framework that learns a query-adaptive graph compression policy, trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization and a joint reward combining downstream question-answering accuracy with token-efficiency constraints, to prune retrieved subgraphs on the fly before they reach the generator. On 200 hard multi-hop questions from HotpotQA, the learned policy achieves an F1-score of 68.8\%, compared with 63.8\% for the uncompressed pipeline, while reducing the average retrieved context from 26,134 to 5,942 tokens (a 58.6\% compression ratio) and consistently outperforming similarity- and heuristic-pruning baselines. These findings indicate that aligning graph compression directly with generator feedback yields more effective and efficient GraphRAG pipelines than static, task-agnostic reduction schemes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mitigates factual hallucination in Large Language Models (LLMs) by grounding their outputs in external document collections. However, conventional RAG operates over flat, chunk-based passages that lack explicit structural relationships, which limits its effectiveness on multi-hop queries. GraphRAG addresses this by organizing text into entity-relation graphs and hierarchical community summaries, enabling more comprehensive reasoning \cite{edge2024local}. Reinforcement learning (RL) has separately proven effective for training sequential decision-making policies over such structures, as demonstrated by agentic multi-turn retrieval frameworks \cite{luo2026graphr1}, offering a natural mechanism for adapting a graph's structure to a specific downstream task rather than relying on fixed heuristics.

Despite this potential, three gaps remain open. First, existing graph reduction and sparsification methods are typically optimized for task-agnostic structural properties, such as degree or density, rather than downstream generation quality. Second, GraphRAG pipelines commonly encode entire retrieved subgraphs or dense community summaries at inference time, incurring substantial token and computational overhead. Third, most compression schemes rely on fixed heuristics or hop-count rules that cannot adapt to the varying semantic complexity of individual queries.

To address these gaps, this paper proposes Adaptive Graph Compression using Reinforcement Learning (AGC-RL), a framework that learns a query-adaptive compression policy aligned directly with downstream question-answering performance. The key contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A reinforcement learning-based, query-adaptive graph compression policy that prunes retrieved subgraphs on the fly rather than applying a fixed reduction rule.
    \item A joint reward function combining downstream question-answering accuracy with token-efficiency constraints, balancing reasoning depth against computational cost.
    \item An empirical evaluation on multi-hop HotpotQA questions showing that the learned policy compresses retrieved context substantially while matching or exceeding the accuracy of the uncompressed pipeline.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section \ref{sec:RW} reviews related work on GraphRAG, graph reduction, and RL-based context optimization. Section \ref{sec:PS} presents the proposed methodology, including the MDP formulation and reward design. Section \ref{sec:ERD} reports experimental results. Section \ref{SEC: limF} discusses limitations, and Section \ref{sec:con} concludes the paper.



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:RW}

Beyond the foundational GraphRAG construction discussed above, a growing body of work targets the efficiency of GraphRAG retrieval and inference without altering the size of the underlying graph. \citet{yu2026graphragr1} proposed GraphRAG-R1, which applies process-constrained RL with a cost-aware reward to guide multi-hop retrieval, and \citet{chen2026pathrag} proposed PathRAG, which prunes redundant relational paths using a fixed flow-based heuristic rather than a learned policy. \citet{song2026kgr1} proposed KG-R1, a single-agent RL framework that reduces inference cost by learning the retrieval trajectory itself, and \citet{fan2026graphragrouter} proposed GraphRAG-Router, which uses a two-stage RL procedure to route queries across heterogeneous GraphRAG-LLM pipelines under a cost-aware reward. In each case, the retrieved graph or hypergraph passed to the generator remains fixed in size; none of these methods reduces the structure of the subgraph after retrieval.

A separate class of work reshapes graph content earlier in the pipeline. \citet{li2026deepgraphrag} proposed Deep GraphRAG, which applies a dynamically weighted reward to textual community summaries to balance local precision against global context, but the reward operates on summary text rather than on the graph topology itself. \citet{hashemi2024comprehensive} surveys graph reduction broadly across sparsification, coarsening, and condensation, but as a survey it does not propose or evaluate an empirical method against a downstream generation task.

Across this literature, no method combines RL-trained structural operators over an already-retrieved subgraph with a reward grounded in downstream GraphRAG answer quality and query-time adaptivity. \section{Methodology}\label{sec:PS}

The workflow of the proposed adaptive graph compression framework is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}. The proposed system included five main phases: i) dataset, ii) data preprocessing and knowledge graph construction, iii) development of the compression policy, iv) compressed answer generation and reward, and v) evaluation of performance. These phases were incorporated to guarantee reproducibility and the prevention of label leakage in the proposed system.

\subsection{Dataset}
The proposed framework was evaluated using HotpotQA \cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, a multi-hop question-answering benchmark in which each answer requires evidence from more than one document. The distractor development set contains 7,405 questions, each paired with ten context passages, a gold answer, and supporting-fact labels. A subset of 200 hard questions was sampled with a fixed random seed of 42, comprising 170 bridge and 30 comparison questions. The supporting-fact labels identify which retrieved content is required, which makes the dataset suitable for studying compression.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
A preprocessing pipeline was used. First, duplicate passages were removed by title, which produced 1,990 unique passages. Second, the gold answers and supporting-fact labels were kept apart from the passage pool so that no label information entered graph construction. The questions were then allocated to training and validation sets in proportions of 80\% and 20\%, respectively. Since the retrieved subgraph sizes were highly skewed, with a median of 48 nodes and a maximum of 845, the split was stratified by subgraph size. The validation set was used only for checkpoint selection and evaluation.

Entities and relations were extracted from each passage with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \cite{qwen2024technical}, and coreferent mentions were merged through name normalization and model-verified entity resolution, producing a knowledge graph $G=(V,E)$ with 14,797 nodes and 15,391 edges.

\subsection{Proposed Framework}
In the proposed framework, a learned compression stage is placed between retrieval and generation. The query and each node are embedded with BGE-M3 \cite{chen2024bgem3}, and their relevance is measured by cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{sim}(q, v_i) = \frac{E_q \cdot E_i}{\lVert E_q \rVert \, \lVert E_i \rVert},
    \label{eq:sim}
\end{equation}
where $E_q$ and $E_i$ denote the embeddings of the query $q$ and node $v_i$. The five most similar nodes are expanded by two hops to form the retrieved subgraph $G_q$, which covered all supporting passages for 171 of the 200 questions.

Each node in $G_q$ is described by four features: query similarity, normalized degree, normalized hop distance from the nearest seed, and a seed indicator. A multilayer perceptron maps these features to the probability of KEEP, with REMOVE as the alternative action. The policy is trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) \cite{shao2024deepseekmath}, where four trajectories are sampled for each question and compared within the group:
\begin{equation}
    A_i = \frac{R_i - \mu_R}{\sigma_R + \epsilon},
    \label{eq:adv}
\end{equation}
where $R_i$ is the reward of trajectory $i$, $\mu_R$ and $\sigma_R$ are the mean and standard deviation of the group rewards, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant. Because a group reward cannot assign credit to individual nodes, a supervised node-level term is added:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} A_i\,\bar{\ell}_i \;-\; \beta\,\bar{H} \;+\; \eta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}},
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $G=4$, $\bar{\ell}_i$ is the log-probability of trajectory $i$ averaged over nodes so that the gradient scale does not depend on subgraph size, $\bar{H}$ is the policy entropy, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}$ is the class-weighted binary cross-entropy against the supporting-fact labels, with $\beta=0.02$ and $\eta=1.0$.

\subsection{Compressed Answer Generation and Reward}
Compression retains every node whose KEEP probability is at least 0.5, yielding the compressed subgraph $G_c \subseteq G_q$. The retained entities, relations, and source passages are passed to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with a 32,768-token context window to generate the answer $A_c$, while the same procedure applied to $G_q$ produces the uncompressed answer $A_o$. Each training trajectory is rewarded as:
\begin{equation}
    R = Q_c + \lambda\, CR \cdot \mathbb{1}\!\left[Q_c \ge Q_o \wedge Q_c > Q_{\varnothing}\right] - \gamma \max(0,\, Q_o - Q_c),
    \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $Q_c$ and $Q_o$ denote the F1-scores of $A_c$ and $A_o$, $Q_{\varnothing}$ is the F1-score with an empty context, $\lambda=0.5$, and $\gamma=2.0$. The compression bonus is granted only when the compressed answer is at least as good as $A_o$ and better than the answer without context, which prevents rewarding the removal of the entire subgraph on questions the model can answer from parametric knowledge.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
Evaluation of the framework was based on answer quality and compression efficiency. Answer quality was measured by Exact Match (EM) and token-level F1-score, whereas the compression ratio Eq.~(\ref{eq:cr}) reflects the fraction of context tokens removed:
\begin{equation}
    CR = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_o},
    \label{eq:cr}
\end{equation}
where $T_o$ and $T_c$ denote the token counts of the uncompressed and compressed contexts. Each KEEP/REMOVE decision was also evaluated as a binary classification against the supporting-fact labels using precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC. The policy was compared with the uncompressed pipeline and two non-adaptive baselines: similarity pruning, which keeps the 50\% of nodes most similar to the query, and heuristic pruning, which keeps the nodes within one hop of a seed.

\section{Results and Discussions}\label{sec:ERD}

This section focuses on evaluating the learning behavior, the node-level decisions, and the contribution of the state features, as well as a quantitative comparison of the proposed policy with the baselines. All the results were evaluated using the 40 validation questions. As presented in Table~\ref{tab:results}, the proposed policy achieved the highest F1-score of 68.8\%, compared with 63.8\% for the uncompressed pipeline, while reducing the average context from 26,134 to 5,942 tokens. Although heuristic pruning used a similar context of 5,908 tokens, it reached an F1-score of only 63.1\%. This means the policy compressed the context by 58.6\% on average without losing answer quality. This is significant because neither non-adaptive baseline exceeded the uncompressed F1-score.

\subsection{Training and Validation Analysis}
Fig.~\ref{fig:training} summarizes the training behavior of the policy. In Fig.~\ref{fig:training}(a), the compression ratio rises at the start of every epoch because the questions were visited in order of increasing subgraph size, and in the first epoch the smallest quarter of subgraphs was compressed less (0.42) than the rest (0.58 to 0.62). Across epochs, the average compression ratio remained between 0.55 and 0.59 without collapsing toward removing the entire subgraph, and the mean entropy decreased only from 0.600 to 0.529. Fig.~\ref{fig:training}(b) shows that the validation F1-score was highest after the first epoch (0.652) and decreased to 0.610, as recall rose at the expense of precision; therefore, the first-epoch checkpoint was retained.

\subsection{Classification Performance and Confusion Matrix}
Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the confusion matrices of heuristic pruning and the proposed policy on the 4,154 validation nodes, 460 of which are relevant. From the figure, heuristic pruning correctly kept 341 relevant nodes while removing 119 of them. In contrast, the policy correctly kept 382 relevant nodes and removed only 78, at the cost of keeping 330 irrelevant nodes instead of 241. The policy therefore reached a higher recall (0.830 against 0.741) and a lower precision (0.537 against 0.586), with nearly equal F1-scores (0.652 and 0.655). This trade-off is consistent with the reward, since removing a supporting node costs more than keeping an irrelevant one.

\subsection{Contribution of the State Features}
The contribution of the state features was examined by training the node classifier with the supervised objective alone and evaluating each representation at the KEEP-rate of heuristic pruning. From Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}, the four structural features alone reached a node-level F1-score of 0.662, whereas adding the 2,048-dimensional node and query embeddings reduced it to 0.518, and projecting them to 64 or 16 dimensions reduced it further to 0.470 and 0.430. Therefore, the raw embeddings add noise at this data scale, and the proposed policy uses only the four structural features.

\subsection{Discussion}
The findings confirm that the policy learned a query-adaptive rule rather than a fixed cut-off. Similar to heuristic pruning, it kept every seed and 95.0\% of the one-hop nodes, but it also selected 4.2\% of the two-hop nodes, recovering 54 of the 119 relevant nodes that any one-hop rule discards. The benefit was concentrated on the 9 questions whose uncompressed context exceeded the 32,768-token window, where the F1-score rose from 0.489 to 0.710, while on the other 31 questions it was unchanged at 0.681. In contrast, heuristic pruning lowered the F1-score by 5.5 points on the 149 of all 200 questions whose context fitted. However, 33 of the 40 validation questions received the same F1-score with and without compression, so the improvement over the uncompressed pipeline was not statistically significant.

\section{Limitations}\label{SEC: limF}

Although the current framework has a strong performance, it bears several limitations that are evident. The policy was developed on 200 HotpotQA questions, and the 40 validation questions were used for both checkpoint selection and evaluation, so no separate test set was available and the answer-level gains were not statistically significant. One quarter of the uncompressed contexts exceeded the 32,768-token window and were truncated. The framework was also evaluated with a single language model and benchmark, and its supervised objective requires supporting-fact labels. Lastly, future research should be done on larger question sets and multiple benchmarks.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:con}

This work elaborates on an adaptive graph compression framework for GraphRAG based on a policy trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization and a supervised node-level objective. Experimental results on 200 questions from the HotpotQA dataset are presented first. The uncompressed GraphRAG pipeline, similarity pruning, and heuristic pruning were employed as baselines and examined within the same experimental framework. After evaluating each of the methods, the proposed policy proved to be the most effective at preserving answer quality under compression, achieving an F1-score of 68.8\% while reducing the context by 58.6\% on average. The analysis also showed that four structural features are sufficient for the policy and that compression is most beneficial when the retrieved context exceeds the window of the language model.

Despite the promising results, there are several directions for future work. Further studies will extend this framework to larger question sets and additional multi-hop benchmarks, evaluate it with other language models, and replace the supporting-fact labels with signals that do not require manual annotation.
